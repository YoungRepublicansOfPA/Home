Who moves, and how taxes change

A century of immigration in the United States

In 1910, about one in seven U.S. residents was foreign-born. That share fell to about one in twenty by 1970 and returned to nearly one in seven in 2022. The Census counts residents who lacked U.S. citizenship at birth, including people who later naturalized.

Pennsylvania recorded a foreign-born share of 16.0% in the 1920 census. The 2020–2024 American Community Survey estimated 7.7%. Each figure counts people living here during its reference period, including people who arrived decades earlier. The modern estimate pools five years of survey responses.

Moves across the Delaware River

Between the 2022 and 2023 IRS filing years, 18,767 matched returns changed address from New Jersey to Pennsylvania. Another 16,075 went the other way. Pennsylvania gained 2,692 returns across this one state line. The returns moving into Pennsylvania covered 29,887 people, compared with 25,412 on returns moving to New Jersey.

The IRS matches addresses across returns. This captures moves by filers and members of their tax households. People who do not file can be missing. The IRS changed its matching method for this period. The records identify the move but do not ask what prompted it.

How a tax incentive changes a household budget

The federal Earned Income Tax Credit supplements earnings for eligible workers. Its value depends on earnings, filing status, and qualifying children. Pennsylvania created a state credit for tax year 2025 equal to 10% of the federal credit. For example, an eligible Pennsylvania filer with a $3,000 federal credit qualifies for a $300 state credit.

The Department of Revenue projected $193 million in relief. Checking the projection requires actual claims for the same tax year. Testing whether the credit affected migration requires later moves and a comparison with similar households facing different rules. The 2022–2023 moves above predate the state credit.
