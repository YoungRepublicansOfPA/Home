Requests for proposals · Project 02 · November 2026

Household battery buffering

Can a Pennsylvania household keep its furnace, refrigerator, sump pump and well running through a storm outage, and buy less power at the most expensive hours, with a battery it owns and can legally connect?

Our RFP series invites Pennsylvanians to turn a household expense into a practical design problem. Here the expense is twofold: the cost of losing power, and the price of the power a home buys. A battery at each home spreads that buffer across the grid instead of concentrating it in one plant. The obstacles include equipment price, a long permitting and utility approval path, installation safety, and the question of whether a battery ever pays for itself. We welcome electricians, solar installers, engineering students and homeowners who can show which obstacle their design removes and what that costs.

Public design challenge. Expressions of interest are welcome on a rolling basis. Funding, purchases and prizes have yet to be committed. Teams should quote a price for each stage so a prospective sponsor can fund a specific, testable result. This page is not legal, electrical or tax advice. A licensed electrician and the local code official decide what can be installed.

The problem in Pennsylvania

How often and how long the power goes out

The Public Utility Commission’s most recent statewide reliability report covers 2024. It counts 71 reportable outage events across the state’s electric distribution companies that year, the most since 1993. PPL alone reported 17, the most for any company in the past 32 years. About 696,292 customers were affected by storms and other “major events” that utilities may exclude from their reliability scores. Most companies missed at least one of their reliability benchmarks.

What a kilowatt-hour costs

The U.S. Energy Information Administration reports an average Pennsylvania residential price of 22.20 cents per kWh in September 2026 (preliminary), up from 20.43 cents in September 2025. That average blends every utility and supplier, so a household’s own bill is the number to use. Source: EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A, release of November 24, 2026 · checked November 24, 2026.

A battery saves money on peak hours only if the household’s rate charges more at those hours. Time-of-use offers, their hours and their prices differ by utility and by supplier. A proposal claiming peak savings must name the tariff it assumes.

The reference design

The reference is a single cabinet that combines a lithium battery, a hybrid inverter and charge controls, the class of system in Signature Solar’s Homestead 60 package. That package pairs an EG4 XR 60 storage unit with 24 solar panels of 600 W each (14.4 kW) and four ground-mount racks. We use it as a size and price reference. What 60 kWh means for a household

At the calculator’s illustrative critical load of about 1 kW (refrigerator, furnace blower, sump pump, lights and internet), 60 kWh covers roughly two days. Running an electric range, water heater or central air conditioner shortens that to hours. The unit is large: a 60 kWh single cabinet is three times the 20 kWh per-unit limit in the model residential code (see Connecting legally). It weighs 1,584 lb, so it must be set with equipment. Smaller households may be better served by a smaller system.

Alternatives in the same class

Smaller and modular: the Enphase IQ Battery 5P stores 5.0 kWh per unit with built-in inverters rated 3.84 kVA continuous, uses lithium iron phosphate, carries a 15-year or 6,000-cycle warranty, is evaluated to UL 9540A and runs grid-connected or in backup. Units are stacked to reach the needed capacity and are paired with Enphase’s combiner and gateway for grid-tied and backup operation. Source: Enphase IQ Battery 5P data sheet, July 2023 edition, checked November 24, 2026. A newer edition may exist.

Other widely sold units, such as Tesla’s Powerwall, are in the same class. We could not open their official spec pages, so we do not list figures for them. Proposals may use any listed equipment.

What to design

Deliver a reproducible plan that a licensed electrician can permit and install at an ordinary Pennsylvania home. It should keep named critical loads running through a 24-hour outage at minimum, report how much longer it can go, and show the household’s cost per kWh delivered. Proposals must address:

  • Sizing for critical loads. List each load, its running and starting power, and its hours of use in an outage. Size the battery in kWh for duration and the inverter in kW for the largest simultaneous load and motor starts. Show the sizing for one winter case with a gas or oil furnace and one with a heat pump.
  • Transfer switching versus whole-home backup. Compare a critical-loads subpanel with a whole-home transfer at the service entrance. Show what each costs, what it keeps running and how the household sheds loads when the battery runs low. The reference unit passes through up to 100 A, so a 200 A service needs a different arrangement or load management.
  • Adding solar. Price a battery-only version that charges from the grid and a version with solar that can recharge during a long outage. State whether the solar exports to the grid, because that decides net metering and the interconnection path.
  • Installation and safety. Specify location, mounting, clearances, separation from doors and windows, protection from vehicles, heat and smoke detection, the emergency disconnect and labeling. Show how a firefighter isolates the system. Include cold-weather behavior: the reference unit is rated down to −13 °F ambient.
  • Grid behavior. Show that the system cannot energize utility lines during an outage, and document the inverter listing and settings the utility will require.
  • Ownership and repair. Supply a one-line electrical diagram, bill of materials with dated quotes, the permit set, commissioning checklist, and the steps for a household to read state of charge, shed loads and restart after a fault.

Use Pennsylvania’s expertise as a resource. Student teams could log a home’s loads for a winter month and compare a small battery sized to measured critical loads with the 60 kWh reference. Participation would require a separate agreement.

Connecting legally in Pennsylvania

Four separate approvals can apply. Which ones a household needs depends on its municipality, its utility and whether the system will send power to the grid. Confirm each with the office named before buying equipment.

1. Building and electrical permits under the Uniform Construction Code

The Uniform Construction Code adopts the 2021 International Residential Code for one- and two-family homes, with Pennsylvania exclusions and amendments, effective January 1, 2026. Our reading of the adopted text found no exclusion of Section R328, the IRC section on energy storage systems. More than 90% of the state’s 2,562 municipalities enforce the code themselves or through a certified third-party agency. In municipalities that opted out, the owner or contractor hires a certified third-party agency for residential work. Ask the municipality who issues the permit. Philadelphia publishes its own code adoption report and may differ. Sources: 34 Pa. Code § 403.21 (amended effective January 1, 2026, 55 Pa.B. 7701) · Department of Labor & Industry, Uniform Construction Code · checked November 24, 2026.

Under the model 2021 IRC Section R328, a residential energy storage system must be listed to UL 9540, installed per its listing and instructions, and spaced at least 3 feet from other units. Individual units are limited to 20 kWh. The total is limited to 40 kWh in utility closets, basements and storage spaces, and 80 kWh in garages, accessory structures, on exterior walls or outdoors on the ground, unless larger ratings are supported by a UL 9540A test report. A 60 kWh single cabinet therefore depends on the code official accepting its UL 9540A documentation. Unverified against the official text. These figures come from UL’s summary of the 2021 residential requirements; the ICC’s free code viewer refused our request. Read 2021 IRC Section R328 and confirm with the local code official. Checked November 24, 2026.

2. National Electrical Code requirements

The City of York’s notice on the 2026 code update lists the 2020 National Electrical Code as the electrical code enforced alongside the 2021 ICC codes. In the 2020 NEC, Article 706 covers energy storage systems, Article 705 covers sources interconnected with the utility, Article 710 covers stand-alone systems and Article 702 covers optional standby systems. Section 230.85 requires an emergency disconnect outside one- and two-family homes. Partly unverified. We could not find a state source stating the NEC edition, and the article numbers are from the 2020 NEC as published, not checked against a Pennsylvania document. Sources: City of York, 2021 UCC code update · NFPA 70 document page · checked November 24, 2026.

3. Utility interconnection under 52 Pa. Code Chapter 75

Chapter 75, Subchapter C sets the interconnection process for customer generation. An inverter-based system rated 10 kW or less can use the Level 1 review if it is certified to IEEE 1547 and UL 1741 and passes screens such as a 20 kW cap on a shared single-phase secondary. Certified inverter systems up to 2 MW use Level 2. The application fee is $100 for Level 1 and $250 plus $1 per kW for Level 2. Each utility runs its own application process and forms, so start with the utility’s interconnection page. Sources: 52 Pa. Code Chapter 75 (§§ 75.22, 75.34, 75.35, 75.37, 75.38) · § 69.2104 · checked November 24, 2026.

Batteries are a gray area. The PUC itself says these 2006 rules do not fully account for battery storage. On December 18, 2025 it voted to begin drafting a proposed rulemaking to modernize them (Docket L-2025-3059032). As of our check, we found comments filed in 2026 but no final rule. How a utility classifies a battery that never exports, and what nameplate it uses for a hybrid inverter, varies by utility. Source: PUC press release, December 18, 2025, checked November 24, 2026.

4. Net metering

A home’s customer-generator system can net meter if its nameplate capacity is 50 kW or less. Generation is credited at the full retail rate up to the household’s use each billing period, and excess carries forward. At the end of the net metering year, June 1 through May 31, the default service provider pays for the remaining excess at its price to compare. Customers who buy from a competitive supplier are credited differently, so check the supplier contract. Net metering is built around generation such as solar. The definitions section does not mention storage or batteries, and a battery charged from the grid is not a generator. How a utility meters stored power that is exported is not settled in the text, so ask the utility. Source: 52 Pa. Code §§ 75.12–75.13, checked November 24, 2026.

5. Incentives as of November 24, 2026

The federal residential clean energy credit is no longer available for new systems. It covered home batteries of at least 3 kWh from 2023. Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, ends it for expenditures made after December 31, 2025. The IRS treats an expenditure as made when installation is completed, so a system paid for in 2025 but installed in 2026 does not qualify. Sources: IRS FAQs on Public Law 119-21, questions 1 and 7 · IRS, Residential Clean Energy Credit · checked November 24, 2026.

State and utility programs: Unverified. We found no statewide Pennsylvania rebate for household batteries, but we could not confirm that from an official list. Some utilities run demand-response or energy-efficiency programs that may involve batteries, and these differ by utility. Proposals that count an incentive must link the program’s official page and its date.

Price and work targets

Test a $10,000 installed target for a system that carries a 1 kW critical load for 24 hours, and report the installed cost per usable kWh. Quote the prototype, one pilot home and runs of 10 and 100 homes separately, including design, permitting, installation, inspection and warranty service. A proposal above target can still be useful if it shows the cost driver and what households gain. The target is our planning figure, not a market price.

As a planning scenario, allow two weeks for load logging and design, four for permitting and utility review, one for installation and inspection, and eight for household trials through at least one storm season. Quote engineering hours, hourly rates, parts, permit fees and outside services by stage.

Calculator defaults: capacity (60 kWh), inverter output (15 kW) and rated cycles (4,533, from 272 MWh of rated throughput ÷ 60 kWh) come from the XR 60 spec sheet. The 15-year service life is the warranty term, not a measured life. The electricity price is the EIA figure above. The installed cost is the Option A total, which includes allowances. Load values, charge level, 90% delivery share, 90% round-trip efficiency and 300 cycles a year are assumptions, not measurements.

What a prototype must demonstrate

Install at three or more homes in at least two utility territories. Simulate a 24-hour outage at each by opening the main breaker with the electrician present. Log critical-load kWh, state of charge, inverter temperature and any load that tripped. Report the hours carried and compare them with the design estimate.

Over a 60-day trial, log kWh charged and discharged with a revenue-grade meter, calculate round-trip efficiency and report the household’s bill change under its actual tariff. Document the permit, inspection and utility approval dates and fees. Show the anti-islanding test result and a supervised demonstration of the emergency disconnect.

How to respond

Submit a public GitHub issue titled “Household battery buffering proposal” with your one-line diagram, the household and loads you designed for, two option prices, backup hours, cost per kWh delivered, permitting and interconnection path, test plan, licensing terms and the specific help you need. Name the utility and municipality you planned for. Include one decision you would leave in the household’s hands, such as which loads to shed first. Please keep private contact details and home addresses out of the public proposal.

Review criteria are safety and code compliance (30%), affordability (30%), backup performance and reliability (20%), and repairability and reproducibility (20%). A review should publish the assumptions and test results that led to its score.
