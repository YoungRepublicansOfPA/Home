Who could vote in Pennsylvania?

In founding-era Pennsylvania, a man could qualify to vote by paying public taxes even if he rented his home. The state's constitution did not impose a general requirement that a voter own land, marry or head a household. Its tax exception for freeholders’ sons helps explain why land ownership appears in accounts of early voting.

How the rules changed

  • 1776. Section 6 extended the vote for representatives to freemen aged 21 or older who had lived in Pennsylvania for a year and paid public taxes during that year. It exempted 21-year-old sons of freeholders from the tax condition. Pennsylvania Constitution of 1776
  • 1790. Pennsylvania required two years’ residence and payment of a state or county tax assessed at least six months before the election. Sons of qualified voters aged 21 to 22 had an exception. Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s account of Article III
  • 1838. Pennsylvania explicitly restricted voting to white freemen. This change excluded Black men who had previously qualified. 1838 Constitution, Article III
  • 1870 and 1920. The Fifteenth Amendment barred the United States and the states from denying or abridging citizens’ voting rights on account of race, color or previous servitude. The Nineteenth Amendment established the corresponding protection on account of sex. Ratified federal amendments
  • 1964 and 1966. The Twenty-fourth Amendment prohibited conditioning the vote in federal elections on payment of a poll tax or other tax. In Harper, the Supreme Court held Virginia’s state-election poll tax violated equal protection. Twenty-fourth Amendment · Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections
  • 1969. In Kramer, an adult resident living in his parents’ home challenged a school-election rule favoring specified property owners, tenants and parents. The Court struck it down under the Fourteenth Amendment. Kramer v. Union Free School District

What would repealing the Nineteenth Amendment change?

The Nineteenth Amendment remains in force. A hypothetical amendment that simply repealed it would remove one federal protection. Repeal alone would leave Pennsylvania’s existing electorate in place. Article VII defines eligibility in terms of citizenship, age and residence, and Article I, section 28 protects equality of rights regardless of sex. The state Election Code sets the voting age at 18, consistent with the federal Twenty-sixth Amendment. Pennsylvania Constitution · Pennsylvania Election Code, section 701

A rule limiting general elections to male landowners who head households would separately exclude women, renters and other adults in the same household. Such a restriction would face the federal Equal Protection Clause and Pennsylvania’s own voting and equality guarantees. A governor or county election board cannot change those guarantees by executive action. The exact legal consequences of a hypothetical amendment would depend on its text.

How constitutional amendments work

Article V allows Congress to propose an amendment with two-thirds support in each chamber. Alternatively, applications from two-thirds of state legislatures (34 today) require Congress to call a proposing convention. Either route requires ratification by three-fourths of the states (38 today), through legislatures or conventions as Congress specifies. The president has no signature or veto role. Article V
